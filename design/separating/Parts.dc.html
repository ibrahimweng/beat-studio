What comes out, and what decides it
Three measurements over one picture of the sound in time and frequency. Between them they answer the only question asked of every moment of the file: which of the four parts does this belong to, and how much of it.
The three measurements
A hit, or a note
A held note is a horizontal line: the same few frequencies, frame after frame. A drum is a vertical one: every frequency at once, for a moment. A median along each axis tells them apart without being told what is playing.
Where it sits
Two channels that agree in level and in timing carry one sound in the middle of the mix, which is usually the lead. Two that disagree are either two sounds or one pushed to a side.
Whether it comes round again
A beat is a loop. Some of a track is the same two or four bars coming back, and some of it is a line that is different every time. The loop's length is found without being told the tempo.
None of the three recognises an instrument, and nothing here can. Two instruments in the same place and the same range do not come apart by this route, and never will.
The four parts
DrumsPEverything that arrives and stops.
BassH × lowThe low end of what is left once the drums are out of it.
LeadH × (1 − low) × frontCentred in the mix, and not part of the loop.
TonalH × (1 − low) × (1 − front)Everything else that is held rather than struck.
The four add back up to the recording. P and H add to one, low and one minus low add to one, and front and one minus front add to one. So every moment of the file is divided rather than damaged, and there is nowhere for a residue to hide. Each part is then written as a 24-bit file, like everything else here, which puts the four within half a step each of exact — a hundred and thirty decibels under the mix.

Rest is a part like the others, because the parts have to add up. It is what no hit and no line accounted for, and how much is in it is the most honest single number about how well this worked on a particular file.
